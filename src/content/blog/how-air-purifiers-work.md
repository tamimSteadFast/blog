Air purifier spec sheets are full of acronyms that sound technical and tell you
very little. This guide explains the handful that actually matter, so you can
look at any unit and know whether it fits your room — and spot the numbers that
are there to impress rather than inform.

## HEPA: what it catches

HEPA is a filtration *standard*, not a brand. A true-HEPA filter captures at
least 99.97% of particles at 0.3 microns in size. That specific size matters
because it's the hardest to catch — particles both larger and smaller are
actually filtered more easily — so a filter that handles 0.3 microns handles
almost everything airborne you care about: dust, pollen, mould spores, and the
fine particles in smoke.

Watch the wording. "HEPA-type" or "HEPA-like" are not the standard; they're
marketing terms for filters that didn't meet it. If the spec sheet won't say
"true HEPA" or quote the 99.97% figure, assume it isn't.

## CADR: how fast it cleans

Clean Air Delivery Rate (CADR) tells you how much clean air a unit produces,
usually in cubic metres or cubic feet per hour. A higher CADR clears a given room
in less time. It's the single most useful number for matching a purifier to a
room — but only if you read it alongside the room size, because a big CADR in a
big room may still be inadequate.

The catch is that CADR and noise rise together. Moving more air means spinning the
fan faster, and a fast fan is a loud fan. That's why the smart move is to buy a
unit with enough CADR to clean your room comfortably on a *lower* setting, rather
than one that only keeps up on full power.

## ACH: how often the air is cleaned

Air Changes per Hour (ACH) ties CADR and room size together. It's how many times
per hour the purifier can process a volume of air equal to your whole room. For
general use, aim for around 4–5 ACH — the air cleaned four to five times an hour.
For allergy or asthma sufferers, higher is better.

To estimate it: take the unit's CADR, divide by your room's floor area, and
adjust for ceiling height. Most manufacturers quote a "recommended room size"
that assumes a comfortable ACH at the unit's *maximum* setting — which is why we
suggest halving that figure if you want to run the purifier quietly.

## Activated carbon: for smells, not particles

HEPA catches particles; it does nothing for odours and gases. That's the job of a
separate activated-carbon filter, which adsorbs smells from cooking, smoke, and
volatile compounds. If odour control matters to you, check that a unit has a real
carbon stage — a thin carbon-coated pre-filter is far less effective than a
substantial carbon layer, and it needs replacing more often.

## Putting it together

When you next read a spec sheet, ignore the adjectives and check four things: is
it *true* HEPA, what's the CADR, what room size does that imply at a realistic
(not maximum) setting, and is there genuine activated carbon if you need it. Those
four answers tell you more than any star rating.
