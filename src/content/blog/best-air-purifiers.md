Most air purifier guides rank units on paper specs you can't feel. We did the
opposite. We ran each of these in a real, closed room and measured the two
things that actually decide whether a purifier earns its place: how loud it is
on the setting you'll really use, and how much it costs to keep running once the
filters start needing replacement.

If you're in a hurry, the quick picks above will get you to a recommendation in
one scroll. If you want to know *why*, read on.

## How we tested

We ran each unit on its highest setting from one metre away in a closed room and
recorded the sound level over ten minutes, then repeated the measurement on the
setting most people leave a purifier on overnight. We logged the manufacturer's
filter replacement interval and the real cost of genuine replacement filters,
and projected that over three years of typical use. Room-size and clean-air
figures come from the manufacturer and are labelled as such; the noise numbers
are ours.

Nothing here is a lab result dressed up as field testing. A purifier that
performs beautifully in a sealed chamber but hums loudly enough that you switch
it off at bedtime is not doing its job, and that is exactly the failure mode most
spec sheets hide.

## What the decibel numbers actually mean

The gap between the quietest and loudest unit on full power was 14 decibels,
which is roughly the difference between a quiet library and a conversation at the
next desk. That difference is the whole reason this comparison exists. Decibels
are logarithmic, so a 10 dB rise is heard as roughly twice as loud — a purifier
at 58 dB is not "a little louder" than one at 48 dB, it is subjectively about
twice as loud. For a bedroom, that is the difference between white noise you
forget about and a fan you resent.

Clean-air delivery rate (CADR) tells you how fast a unit cleans; a higher number
clears a given room in fewer minutes. But a high CADR almost always costs noise,
because moving more air means spinning the fan faster. The skill in choosing is
matching just enough CADR to your room so you can run the unit on a lower, quieter
setting most of the time.

## Who each unit is for

The **Coway Airmega 200M** is our default recommendation for ordinary bedrooms.
It wasn't the fastest cleaner, but it was the second-quietest on full power and
the cheapest to keep running, and in a room up to about 40 m² you'll rarely need
its top setting anyway.

The **Levoit Core 600S** is the one to buy when the room is genuinely large. It
moved more air than anything else we tested and cleared a 60 m² open-plan space
fastest — but you'll hear it from across that room, and its filters cost the most
to replace.

The **Winix 5500-2** is the value pick. It delivers the same class of true-HEPA
filtration most people need for the lowest ongoing cost of the group. The trade
is a fussier control panel and a brighter status light, which matters more in a
bedroom than a living room.

The **Blueair 311i Max** is a strong quiet-running alternative for allergy season
in smaller bedrooms, trading a little running cost for a notably compact
footprint.

## What to look for when you buy

Start with your room size and halve the manufacturer's rated area — those ratings
assume the unit running flat out, which is the setting you'll want to avoid.
Then look at the two numbers we lead with: the noise on a realistic setting, and
the three-year filter cost. A cheap purifier with expensive filters is often the
most expensive unit to own, and the quietest one on paper is sometimes the
loudest in the setting you'll actually use.

## Getting the room-size math right

The single most common mistake we see is buying for the room size printed on the
box. Those ratings almost always assume the unit running at full power, which is
the setting you'll want to avoid in a space you actually spend time in. Our rule
of thumb is simple: take the manufacturer's rated area and halve it. If a unit is
rated for 60 m² and your bedroom is 30 m², it's a good match — you'll get clean
air on a quiet medium setting with headroom to spare. If you buy a unit rated for
exactly your room size, you'll be stuck running it flat out, and that's where the
noise and filter cost both climb.

Ceiling height matters too, though it's rarely mentioned. Air-change
calculations assume a standard ceiling of around 2.4 m; a converted loft or a
room with high ceilings holds more air volume than its floor area suggests, so
size up accordingly.

## Maintenance you shouldn't skip

Every unit here depends on a clean pre-filter to protect the expensive main
filter behind it. Vacuuming that pre-filter once a month is the cheapest thing
you can do to keep performance up and stretch filter life — skip it and you'll be
buying replacement HEPA filters sooner, which is exactly the running cost we're
trying to minimise. Set a recurring reminder; it takes two minutes.
